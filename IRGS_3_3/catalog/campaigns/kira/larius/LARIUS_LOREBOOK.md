# Larius: The Weight of Living History

Adopted setting canon · The World of Kira v0.5 · reference present: 1342 A.A.

This book is the current setting canon for Larius. Kieran Pelleas is the continent’s main character: the recurring figure whose life connects its histories and present struggles. The expanded cultures, religions, institutions, supporting people and the Long Renewal have been adopted by the world’s creator. The older documents are a superseded creative archive. Possible future stories remain possibilities, with their outcomes left to later authorship or play.

## 1. The continent that cannot agree on its past

An Akarian schoolchild learns that the Ascension brought an ancient people into daylight. A Sadire child learns that it brought a war. In Everwood it marks the end of a thousand years of republican peace. In Estru and Kyrinus, people ask why the calendar still honours the moment when another nation appeared above them.

All of them use the same dates.

Larius is a continent where history remains part of ordinary life. A market charter may rest on a peace whose signatories are dead, while the elf arguing its meaning met them. A human family may have tended a farm for generations on land that another household still remembers losing. A dwarven factory can manufacture the weapons that once terrified its customers’ ancestors. The same trade can feed a town and make its neighbours afraid.

The continent’s five political cultures give the conflict sharper forms. Akaria trusts industry, armoured force and the superiority of its own imperial order. Sadion combines a legionary ideal of organized service with the layered rights and rival powers of a medieval realm. Everwood celebrates a magically powerful civilization while ranking its own diverse elven population by hereditary sub-race. Kyrinus answers with a humbler, more accepting culture of trade. Estru asks how a divided people can resist neighbours powerful enough to turn dependence into a system.

None of those answers is entirely false. None serves everyone equally.

The tone of Larius is heroic fantasy grounded in difficult obligations. Great deeds matter. A bridge held against raiders, a plague ward supplied through winter or a treaty rescued from its own wording can change lives. Power does not excuse the holder from consequences. A noble motive can produce a ruin that survives for centuries.

Kieran Pelleas stands at the meeting of these ideas. To much of Larius he is the old ranger who has already lived through its greatest arguments. To himself, he is a man who has repeatedly failed to finish learning from them.

## 2. A history of foundations, wounds and unfinished settlements

### Before the Ascension

The oldest histories concern peoples becoming nations. Barvaris Akarian united the dwarven clans around 3000 B.A., using diplomacy where it worked and conquest where it did not. Later imperial teaching celebrates the achievement as the First Joining. Families descended from absorbed clans sometimes use another name: the Closing of the Small Doors. Both names belong to the same history, remembered from different positions.

Everwood’s earlier peoples formed Evalia and Woadaris, fought, and were brought together as Evermore. Their early strength did not protect them from the disease crisis associated with the Sadire migrations around 2500 B.A. The catastrophe broke confidence in natural elven resilience. Its lasting legacy is a distrust of anyone who claims that ancestry alone guarantees safety. It also left an uglier inheritance: periodic attempts to blame living human neighbours for a calamity their distant ancestors neither understood nor intended.

The lands that became Estru also have a history that a Sadire colonial account alone cannot tell. Desert Elves were initially unwilling participants in the society associated with Sadire settlement. Across the centuries they helped form Estru’s culture; they are part of its foundations, rather than a population added after an otherwise complete human nation was made. The exact circumstances of that initial unwillingness and the stages of cultural change remain undated.

The Sadire histories remember migration, settlement, the growth of Bay Watch and the kings Grad and San Vollan. Their dates remain less certain than their national importance. Grad represents the making of order; San, the idea that order must become institutions able to outlive a ruler. Later generations argue over both men because invoking a founder is easier than proving that a present policy serves anyone.

The Evalian civil war began around 1010 B.A. The monarchy fell, King Carius was executed, and a representative republic replaced the old throne. Kieran’s birth is placed in 1000 B.A., at the war’s end, to the first prime minister and the deposed king’s daughter. This makes him a child of the settlement itself. Royal ancestry and republican legitimacy meet in his family without ceasing to disagree.

The old capital at Tethris remains important to royal memory. Evalia is the republican capital; the old map’s Evalian label refers to that same city.

### The Ascension and the long war

At the calendar’s turning, dwarves reached the snowy surface above their underground empire. In Akarian memory this was a discovery of possibility. For their neighbours it became an encounter with a highly organized people whose ambitions were difficult to understand and harder to resist.

The national histories place the invasion of Estru and Kyrinus in 80 A.A. And the peace in 400 A.A. Dwarven advances in 110 A.A. Brought the colonies close to defeat. The Evalian–Sadire alliance of 200 A.A. Began recovering the occupied lands. The eventual peace left territories and compensation arranged by the states powerful enough to negotiate them.

Kieran’s account centres on a frontier encounter in which one of his scouts fired at dwarves, the destruction of his unit and the fall of Cthulis. He carries responsibility for his command. It does not follow that a frightened patrol alone created every imperial ambition, military preparation or colonial grievance that produced the wider war. One of his hardest lessons is to distinguish responsibility from the belief that every catastrophe was his to prevent.

The continental war lasted 320 years, from 80 to 400 A.A. Kieran’s active wartime service occupied 228 of those years. His service and the wider conflict are remembered on different scales: withdrawal from command did not end the war, and peace did not end its consequences.

### Independence without reconciliation

In 500 A.A., the anger of Estru and Kyrinus became rebellion. Their former rulers defeated them and then allowed independence. The contradiction matters: each new nation inherited both the right to govern itself and the memory that it had not won on its own terms.

Kieran’s flight from Kyrinus belongs to this crisis. He gathered elves, humans and dwarves who did not wish to become enemies, fought a way to the docks and stole a ship. The people who remained together afterward formed the Rangers of Larius. Their first great achievement was an evacuation rather than a conquest.

In 600 A.A., Estru attacked Sadion and was defeated. Kyrinus refused to join, then rebuilt relations with Everwood. Subsequent generations have often mistaken an absence of continental war for a resolution of its causes. Trade resumed; public grief became ceremony; uncomfortable records moved into archives.

The period since then is called the Long Peace in this edition. It contains border incidents, private violence, labour disputes, failed harvests and local emergencies. Calling it peaceful means that the principal states have avoided repeating the old general war. It does not mean that every life has been safe.

### The present: 1342 A.A.

Cathius Akarian seeks prosperity through trade and underground expansion. Erik Vollan’s popularity gives Sadion a confident court and an awkward question about what follows him. Everwood’s caste-bound republic guards its prestige and magical predominance. Kyrinus benefits from renewed relations while rejecting much of the old homeland’s insularity. Southern Estru trades with Akaria, and its position within the wider Estrusian settlement remains a subject for further political development.

The last surviving scene follows Kieran from his camp toward Castle LuCain after an offer to join a westward exploration fleet. In the expansion, its importance is larger than a new voyage. It offers the states of Larius somewhere to place hopes they have stopped finding at home. Whether they will carry their oldest habits with them is an open question.

## 3. Akaria: the armoured empire

Akaria believes that industry, endurance and force have earned it the right to command respect. Its most conspicuous answer to a foreign objection is a dwarf in heavy armour, mounted on a great Battle Ram beneath a banner whose maker can recite an imperial lineage. Its courtiers expect a visitor to notice the banner before they are willing to discuss the objection.

The creator’s military references are Sasanian and Byzantine heavy cavalry; the social reference is an insular, status-conscious medieval England after William’s conquest. These influences produce an Akarian order of imperial ceremony, powerful landed and industrial households, aggressive military prestige and strong suspicion of outsiders. They do not replace dwarven history with the literal history of any real empire.

The empire’s great houses justify privilege through service, ancestry and the conquests that made their holdings possible. Lesser clans may retain their own honours while remembering how submission entered their family history. Rights are layered: an imperial grant, an old clan compact and a workshop charter may all govern different parts of the same dispute. A powerful house can be an employer, landlord, military patron and court connection at once.

Insularity has practical forms. Foreign visitors may receive lavish hospitality while remaining unable to enter a workshop, inspect an account or acquire standing in a local court without an approved sponsor. The compliment “remarkably accomplished for an outsider” reveals the prejudice beneath the ceremony. Familiar foreigners can become exceptions without the speaker revising the rule.

Akarians are industrious as well as aggressive. Long projects, disciplined craftsmanship and the maintenance of complex underground works are sources of national pride. Success strengthens a belief that other peoples ought to acknowledge Akarian superiority. Workmanship can be excellent while the terms on which it is sold are coercive. An empire can protect its own workers and still treat a weaker neighbour as a market it is entitled to control.

### The mailed ram and the imperial lance

The Bracatis mounted tradition gives that culture a striking military expression. Large Battle Rams carry heavily armoured riders whose preferred battlefield role is to deliver a concentrated shock against an enemy line. The elite companies are called the Iron Lances. Their prestige draws on the Sasanian and Byzantine image of armoured cavalry: expensive preparation, coordinated action and riders whose social standing is visible in their equipment.

Armour can protect the ram’s head, chest and vulnerable approaches without making every mount an identical moving fortress. Breeders, handlers, armourers and farriers support the spectacle. Wealth and inherited access to suitable animals help preserve elite status. An unmounted craftsman who made an officer’s equipment may still be expected to yield the road to him.

The cavalry is formidable, not an automatic answer to every battlefield. Broken formations, poor ground, obstacles, disrupted approach and the needs of living animals matter. Infantry, missile troops and firearms create the conditions in which a charge can be useful; logistics keep the force capable of reaching a battle at all. No exact formation size, combat value or universal charge outcome is selected by this lore.

Cathius’s discovery and taming of Malachi founded the modern mounted division. The present splendour of the ram companies grew from that comparatively recent institution, supported by older aristocratic ideals of martial service. Their handlers and breeders are still building traditions that ambitious courtiers already describe as timeless.

### Pomp, work and the limits of belonging

Court life delights in precedence: who enters first, which ancestral deed appears on a seal, which household may stand closest to the throne. Public armour, worked metal, formal braids and embroidered caparisons turn wealth into a visible argument about rank. Arrogance need not mean incompetence. An officer can be genuinely skilled and insufferably certain that foreign soldiers exist principally to illustrate the superiority of his training.

The household, workshop and clan remain important, but belonging is not equally available to everyone. Adoption within accepted Akarian circles can carry standing; admission of an outsider faces much stronger resistance. The empire’s ten underground cities and five surface cities give this culture different settings. Surface families may depend on trade with people whom deep-city relations dismiss, while still sharing much of the same national pride.

The First Hearth religion gives sound work and inherited obligation sacred language. Its imperial establishment can treat the empire as the proper shelter of a specially burdened people. Reformers answer that shelter has become an excuse to close the door. The Hearth Leagues organize mutual aid and labour petitions, but their members are not automatically cosmopolitan: a man who demands fair treatment in a factory may resent a foreign apprentice receiving the same protection.

Cathius is a meaningful individual within this society. He is calm, stern, caring and conscious of ancestral mistakes, with a preference for underground expansion and trade. He does not need to be rewritten as a reckless conqueror to lead an aggressive empire. His challenge is to redirect its pride without appearing ashamed of it. Even his reforms may preserve privileges he considers natural. His friendship with Kieran is exceptional enough to attract admiration, resentment and careful calculation at court.

### Dominion over someone else’s viable homeland

The creator establishes that dwarves hold dominion over the region suitable for Ice Elven habitation. Most of Larius is unsuitable for those elves, making Akarian control a constraint on where they can live. Their stronger magic does not remove that dependence on place. This is a present geographical and political relationship; the creator has not supplied a conquest, expulsion or treaty explaining how it arose.

Access to viable habitat is a source of political tension. A proposed local agreement could concern settlement, winter passage, shelter and the use of a valley already claimed by an Akarian house. Ice Elven negotiators would be discussing the conditions of viable residence; an imperial official might regard the same request as an ordinary petition for permission. Each could leave the meeting believing the other had refused to recognize the central issue.

Cathius’s preference for underground expansion makes negotiation plausible without making him the owner of a simple solution. A grant from the emperor can meet resistance from households whose rights he otherwise defends. An Ice Elven community can seek workable terms without conceding that its future should depend on one ruler’s generosity. No existing agreement, settlement location or ruler over the Ice Elves is established by this proposal.

Food and domestic hospitality retain the practical habits of underground life: cultivated fungi, stored roots, preserved vegetables, strong broths and surface cheeses. A grand household may display imported luxuries while praising self-sufficiency. The contradiction bothers visitors more readily than their hosts.

## 4. Everwood: the republic of unequal branches

Everwood is a republic that considers itself the custodian of a civilization other peoples have not yet learned to equal. Its institutions are old, its manners exacting and its magical achievements genuinely formidable. Those achievements have become a justification for withdrawing from outsiders and arranging even its own elven population into hereditary ranks.

The creator’s French reference supplies courtly prestige, elaborate etiquette, patronage, heraldic display and confidence in a distinctive high culture. It is an authoring influence, not a request for comic accents or a factual claim about real French people. Everwood remains the fictional Grand Republic of Evalia, with its own history, institutions and failings.

### A caste society within a diverse people

The creator establishes a caste system based on elven sub-race. The regional list names Plains, Sea, Forest, Mountain, Ice and Desert elves, but ancestry is not a map of residence or citizenship. Desert Elven communities live in Evalia as well as Estru and Kyrinus; Ice Elves are primarily isolationists constrained by unsuitable habitat across much of the continent. Everwood’s mingling communities do not all have equal public standing, and its institutions do not necessarily encompass every elven community in Larius. An academy, assembly chamber or great household can contain several sub-races while still arranging their authority around inherited classification.

Caste can shape admission to prestigious magical teaching, eligibility for important offices, acceptable marriage negotiations, access to patrons and the weight accorded a petitioner’s account. A person may be welcomed as a useful specialist and excluded from the room in which the specialists’ future is decided. Courtesy makes the distinction easier to preserve, not less consequential.

The creator places Sea elves in the highest recognized caste because of their retained magical superiority within that social tradition. The later clarification also establishes that Ice Elven magic exceeds even theirs. The caste claim therefore cannot be read as proof that Sea Elves are the most magically capable elven people everywhere in Larius. Plains and Tree elves are the most numerous and form the majority of society. Although Sea elves feel superior to them, they recognize those two groups as equals. Tree elves are called Forest elves in the regional peoples list; both labels are retained here.

This is not a simple ladder with Sea elves ruling an undifferentiated lower population. Sea elves hold the greatest magical and ceremonial prestige, while the numerous Plains and Tree communities possess recognized peer standing and much of the society’s ordinary institutional weight. Magical precedence, personal condescension and recognition as an equal can coexist, however uncomfortable the distinction looks to an outsider.

Mountain elves are the least numerous. Their strength makes them valued soldiers in wars against Humans and Dwarves, but their lesser magical capability leaves them looked down upon. The contradiction is visible when a community celebrates a Mountain company’s service while declining to treat its members as equals away from the battlefield. They have substantial skill and histories of their own; their social standing is not a measure of their inherent worth.

The exact caste positions of Ice and Desert Elves, and the treatment of mixed-sub-race households, remain unassigned. Their cultural and geographical identities are now clearer: Desert Elves helped form Estru, while Ice Elven isolation is strongly shaped by the scarcity and political control of viable habitat. Neither fact supplies an automatic legal rank in Evalia. The republic has not erased the elves’ superiority complexes. Its exact rules of office, voting, education and legal status must be developed around the creator’s distinctions rather than used to flatten them into either universal equality or a rigid ranking the creator did not supply.

Caste is a political and social hierarchy, not the narrator’s proof of anyone’s intrinsic worth. Real differences in inherited traits do not demonstrate that the ruling classification is fair, that every individual fits its stereotype or that an exceptional practitioner belongs to an approved lineage. Officials can nonetheless use genuine magical differences to make their claims appear beyond argument.

### The great magical civilization

The elves remain the most powerful practitioners of magic among Larius’s peoples, as specified by the creator. Everwood’s diversity helps sustain a broad magical tradition. The old accounts associate Sea elves with particular magical strength. The creator now places Ice Elven magic above even Sea Elven magic, while retaining Sea Elven caste precedence in Evalia. A republic’s hierarchy and the full range of elven capability are different things. The setting gives their schools, households and local traditions different specialties without converting sub-race into an automatic spell list.

Great families sponsor teachers, collections, crafted wonders and public demonstrations of mastery. Patronage can preserve knowledge across centuries while making access dependent on approval. A talented lower-caste pupil may become a celebrated proof of a patron’s generosity rather than an argument against the system that required a patron at all.

Isolationism is therefore more than a closed border. It is a confidence that contact is worthwhile chiefly when it confirms what Everwood already believes. Foreign ambassadors can be entertained exquisitely, guided through approved wonders and sent home without having been treated as intellectual equals. Merchants and officials maintain necessary connections even while public rhetoric celebrates distance from lesser societies.

### The Ice Elves beyond the republic’s measure

Ice Elves mature faster and live slightly less long than the Sea Elves with whom the creator compares their magic. Their ordinary reference lifespans are about 870 years for Ice Elves and 900 for Sea Elves. Faster maturation does not turn Ice Elves into a short-lived people; an exact maturity age remains undeveloped. Individual talent, education and accomplishment still vary.

Their isolation has an ecological and political basis. Most of Larius is unsuitable for their habitation, and dwarves have dominion over the suitable region. This makes contact costly and the conditions of residence consequential. It does not establish that they never travel, that every community has the same policy, or that they are subjects of Evalia merely because an Evalian catalogue lists them.

Authority within an Ice Elven community rests partly on maintaining conditions in which the next generation can actually remain. Shelter keepers, teachers and negotiators gain standing through continuity of care. Their magic can sustain remarkable accomplishments while dependable supplies, safe approaches and agreements with neighbours remain essential. A skilled shelter keeper can command respect without claiming rule over every Ice Elven community.

Ice Elven hospitality distinguishes an invitation to visit from permission to settle. Hosts explain the practical limits of a stay before offering ceremonial hospitality; accepting an honoured guest need not commit a small community to an indefinite burden. Younger adults, reaching maturity sooner, can challenge elders over how long caution should delay a useful connection. The shorter lifespan is slight, but the question of how much of one’s adult life to spend waiting can still be sharp.

For Evalian elites, the Ice Elves pose an uncomfortable question: what does a claim to magical supremacy mean when stronger practitioners have little reason to seek their approval? Some officials might admire them, court their teaching or dismiss their absence as irrelevance. Those reactions are possible character positions, not an established continental consensus or a new caste assignment.

### A republic without social equality

The monarchy’s fall did not abolish every hereditary advantage. In this interpretation, the republic ended one family’s exclusive claim to the throne while preserving many inherited claims to education, prestige and the right to speak for others. Its assembly can be proud of representation and selective about whose voice counts as representative.

The republic’s founding constitution provided for city and village representatives, quarterly meetings and 256 seats. That founding apportionment does not establish the population or exact seat allocation of the present day. The caste system must eventually be reconciled with the exact workings of office and representation, but its existence does not require replacing the republic with a monarchy.

Some lower-status families defend the order because their local privileges depend on it. Some high-status elves criticize it because they have witnessed its waste and cruelty. More common are people who recognize an injustice in private and find a polite reason to avoid disturbing the institution that benefits them. The society should contain disagreement without losing its established isolationist and superiorist character.

### Beauty, memory and Kieran’s place

Formal life turns accomplishment into display: exact manners, cultivated speech, intricate dress, commissioned music, renowned craft and the expectation that a guest will understand an allusion without explanation. The absence of that knowledge can be used to distinguish the supposedly uncultivated from the merely unfamiliar. Provincial communities have their own pride and may resent metropolitan judgements even while reproducing caste distinctions locally.

The Turning Bough religion is claimed by an establishment that interprets different branches as different inherited duties and ranks. Reforming teachers insist that all branches support the same living tree. Neither interpretation is presented as an objective cosmic endorsement of social inequality.

Kieran is both a product of this civilization and a difficulty within it. His mother’s royal descent, his father’s republican standing and his own extraordinary magic grant him access denied to many of the people he might defend. His travels give him reasons to doubt Everwood’s claims without erasing the defensive elven pride recorded in his character sheet. His story becomes stronger when he must notice the doors that opened for him before deciding what to do about those kept shut for others.

## 5. Sadion: the medieval kingdom with a legionary memory

Sadion combines Roman and Holy Roman design. The Legion of Valiance preserves the language, discipline and tactical ambitions of a legionary tradition, while the soldiers and nobles inhabit a medieval material world influenced by the Holy Roman Empire. Its emblematic soldier should not be mistaken for an ancient Roman legionary dressed unchanged across the centuries.

The house of Vollan gives the kingdom a central dynasty and a story of public order. Roman influence appears in ceremonial standards, disciplined marching, the honour attached to a unit’s name and the idea that organized service holds a realm together. Holy Roman influence appears in powerful regional houses, local privileges, chartered communities and competing legal claims beneath the crown. Royal power remains vested in King Erik; the kingdom’s precise constitutional arrangements remain further development.

### The Legion of Valiance

The Legion’s military ideal is coordinated formation fighting. Units are trained to keep order, obey signals, support neighbouring formations and retain reserves rather than spend every soldier in a single impressive rush. Reconnaissance, prepared camps, fieldworks, route security and reliable supply belong to its conception of competence. The Roman reference concerns institutional memory and broad tactical practice, not a claim that every ancient formation works unchanged against Larius’s magic and firearms.

Equipment follows the creator’s medieval Holy Roman reference. Mail, protective coats, helmets and more extensive plate appear according to role and means; spears, polearms, swords, shields and crossbows are natural design elements. Mounted nobles and specialist cavalry can support the infantry tradition without displacing it. The exact development and distribution of these forms remain authored setting details, not a new universal equipment package.

The old character records keep their actual equipment and ranks. Daniel’s bronze sabers and Dole Company’s captains, lieutenants and sergeants are not erased to make the whole force imitate a Latin textbook. If a later military catalogue adds cohorts or other higher formations, they must fit those inherited records rather than silently replacing them. No exact unit strengths or battlefield bonuses are selected here.

What commanders admire is the ability to hold, reform and advance together. What propagandists admire is a picture of perfect obedience. The difference permits stories about sound discipline, stubborn incompetence and officers who mistake punishment for training. Local contingents may carry strong regional loyalties even while serving under the Legion’s wider name.

### Crown, charter and regional power

The early kings provide different models of legitimate rule. Grad made a realm; San built enduring institutions; Kali defended Sadion and crushed Estru’s invasion. Modern patrons choose the founder whose example best serves their present argument. A noble protecting a hereditary privilege and a town defending its charter may both claim to uphold ancient law against innovation.

Erik is the current popular king, with Alek identified as his son. His ability to coordinate the realm depends on relationships as well as reputation. Great households, officers, local courts, wealthy towns and religious establishments can cooperate with the crown while guarding their own rights. Popularity does not make every demand easy to enforce or every local grievance imaginary.

The political culture values service and recognized standing. A family can gain honour through a military career, prosperous craft or a public office, but those achievements do not dissolve inherited distinctions. Regional assemblies, festivals and courts provide settings in which rank is displayed, contested and negotiated. Their exact constitutional powers remain to be defined rather than borrowed wholesale from any one historical century.

### Common life and the imperial style

Sadire public architecture favours substantial gates, formal approaches, assembly spaces and visible emblems of the crown or unit that paid for them. A ceremonial procession can evoke an imperial past while passing workshops and market stalls that belong to a thoroughly medieval present. Merchants, clerks and veterans take pride in being part of a civilization organized enough to remember an obligation.

Rural life varies with landholding and local privilege. A free farmer, tenant, estate steward and hired worker may share a feast while facing very different choices after a failed harvest. Towns provide other routes to influence. Griffin’s crowded lodging houses and markets produce resourcefulness and solidarity alongside the exploitation found in Daniel’s childhood account.

The Lantern Covenant gains a more formal public face in this revision: processions, unit blessings, patronal observances, established houses and arguments over whether the crown has fulfilled its sacred duties. Its worship remains a fictional religion, not a literal importation of either Roman state religion or medieval Christianity. Grand ceremony can coexist with a village house whose survival depends on one family’s flour.

Sadion’s central tension is between the claim that service makes the realm one body and the unequal power of its members. A commander wants reliable obedience. A soldier’s household wants the kingdom to remember its promises. A town proud of the Legion may refuse to let a noble use the same soldiers to erase its charter.

## 6. Kyrinus: a more open elven civilization

The Karthinians retain elven longevity and magical inheritance without accepting that these entitle them to treat every other people as an inferior audience. The creator describes a more accepting, humbler society with the trader’s outlook of medieval Italians. Independence from Everwood has become an opportunity to build a different style of elven public life.

Medieval Italian influence appears in commercial households, maritime connections, civic rivalry, guild patronage, banking, negotiated law and practical diplomacy. It does not make every city a copy of Venice, Genoa or Florence, or provide a final constitutional plan for the Karthinian Council. The council is inherited from Kieran’s record; its exact composition remains open.

### Courtesy as a practical strength

A Karthinian host is expected to learn what matters to a guest before demonstrating how much the host already knows. Traders who cannot listen lose useful relationships. The resulting humility is a public ideal and a learned skill, not a magical guarantee that every citizen is kind. A merchant can be courteous, well informed and determined to strike a hard bargain.

Compared with Everwood, ancestral sub-race is less readily accepted as a sufficient explanation of a person’s worth or entitlement. Civic practice gives more weight to a record of work, a sponsor’s credibility, an agreement kept and the usefulness of knowledge. Foreign residents and mixed elven circles are more readily integrated into ordinary life. Specific rights and offices still need legal definition; an accepting culture does not automatically erase differences of wealth or citizenship.

Desert Elves are among Kyrinus’s resident communities, as the creator confirms. They are not automatically expatriate Estrusians or Evalian visitors; shared ancestry can connect households without determining citizenship. Their society gives such households opportunities in civic life as well as commerce: a teacher, harbour official or neighbourhood organizer need not exist principally to interpret another people’s culture. Their exact legal standing remains to be developed.

The relationship with Everwood is consequently more than a dispute between identical societies. Some Karthinians regard the old homeland’s prestige with fascination; others see its caste etiquette as a habit they escaped. An Evalian visitor may find hospitality comforting until discovering that the host does not intend to recognize the visitor’s inherited precedence.

### Cities of exchange

The setting contains merchant houses, craft associations, shipping partnerships, brokers and keepers of accounts. A family can invest in a voyage, sponsor a chapel or Horizon house, maintain kin abroad and argue fiercely over the terms offered by its own relatives. Civic pride appears in ports, public buildings, festivals and the patronage of useful or beautiful work.

Trade requires more than taking a profit. Navigation, trusted weights, language, credit, adjudication, storage and knowledge of unfamiliar customs all support a transaction. Those skills give people without royal descent ways to become influential. They also let established families control opportunities while claiming that everyone succeeds only through merit.

The Open Horizon fits this environment as a faith of freely undertaken obligations, hospitality and responsibility beyond one’s own shore. Its houses can witness contracts and support the stranded without turning a successful negotiation into proof of divine approval. A courteous bargain can still be exploitative when one side has nowhere else to go.

### Independence, wealth and Kieran

The trade agreement and non-aggression pact with Everwood are genuine achievements. Kyrinus can seek continued connection without accepting renewed subordination. Commercial interests sometimes welcome arrangements that opponents consider too dependent on the old homeland; the disagreement need not make every advocate of trade unpatriotic.

Vlaris carries Kieran’s private memory of withdrawal and ordinary work. Cthulis carries his memory of command, resistance and evacuation. Their residents can admire him without accepting that gratitude grants him a permanent right to supervise their future. A more open society is particularly well placed to expose how often he has mistaken his own generous intentions for another person’s consent.

The name Karthinian is retained exactly as the creator’s identity for these elves. It is Estru’s culture, not Kyrinus’s, that has the stated Carthaginian influence. The similarity of names does not change the supplied cultural map.

## 7. Estru: the divided heirs

The Estrusian human population descends from the Sadire, but Estru’s culture was also formed by its long-resident Desert Elves. Its national identity cannot be explained solely as a human colony becoming independent. Both the human inheritance and Desert Elven participation belong in its founding story. Their political reference is medieval German fragmentation before unification: rival local powers, cities, households and leagues unable to assume the protection of a single overwhelming state. Their cultural references are the ancient Etruscans and Carthaginians. These are different layers of the same society, not alternative labels to choose between.

The creator establishes Estru as oppressed by larger and more powerful neighbours. That pressure belongs in the ordinary functioning of its towns: unfavourable trading terms, demands dressed as protection, border intimidation, foreign patrons with local clients and the need to bargain for room to act. The precise legal forms require further definition, but dependence is not merely an outsider’s misunderstanding of an otherwise equal relationship.

### Desert Elves and the making of Estru

The creator describes Desert Elves as initially unwilling residents in the history of Sadire settlement, with centuries separating those beginnings from the present. They helped form Estru culturally as it emerged from that colonial history. No exact first-settlement date or legal instrument is supplied. Initial unwillingness establishes a difficult origin without deciding whether every household experienced the same coercion or how later generations understood their place.

Estru’s culture formed through unequal contact, adaptation and the development of shared institutions. Desert Elven participation is visible in ordinary civic life, rather than confined to an exotic quarter or a forgotten ancestor. Houses built for shade and shared courtyards, the keeping of communal water, craft teaching and the recitation of household obligations carry Desert Elven contributions into Estrusian habits shared across ancestry. Their importance varies by city and household; no single profession or temperament defines every Desert Elf.

Shared culture does not make the origins consensual after the fact. A city can take pride in a public court whose early building obligations were imposed. A household can call itself Estrusian and still object to a national ceremony describing Sadire settlers as the only founders. Some people may insist on restitution, some on recognition, and some on the security of present work and kin. Those are possible positions within a living population, rather than a single grievance all Desert Elves must hold.

Desert Elves also live in Kyrinus and Evalia. Family ties and correspondence can connect those communities without giving them identical loyalties or making Estru the government of all Desert Elves. An Evalian Desert Elf may regard a cousin’s civic standing in Estru with interest; the cousin may envy access to an academy while rejecting its social assumptions. Neither must choose between being a real elf and belonging to the society in which they live.

### Many powers, insufficient shelter

The setting develops a landscape of proud cities, territorial households, temple establishments and temporary leagues. They can cooperate against a common threat and still dispute precedence, debt and who must pay for the cooperation. A prosperous city may prefer an agreement with a foreign patron to a costly league led by its nearest rival.

Southern Estru and the Estrusian Protectorate are names used within the continent’s unsettled political order. The political inspiration supports a plural, pressured society but does not settle its final constitution, partition, ruler or border map. Calling a region a protectorate must eventually specify whose protection, what obligations and whose consent are involved.

Estru’s defeat in the war of 600 A.A. Remains part of its history. Present oppression does not require denying the agency or mistakes of past Estrusian rulers. It does explain why neighbours can repeatedly use an old defeat to justify present demands, and why appeals to national dignity can both unite communities and conceal the interests of their patrons.

### The culture of the old cities

Etruscan influence informs a strong concern with ancestry, sacred interpretation, ceremonial authority, worked bronze and the visible continuity of a household. Painted family tombs and commemorative feasts make the dead part of civic identity. Rich cloth, decorated vessels and carefully staged hospitality can express a dignity that a foreign embassy refuses to acknowledge politically.

Carthaginian influence develops the mercantile side: trading households, maritime ambition, civic patronage, temple wealth, commercial networks abroad and an ability to negotiate across cultural boundaries. Some families care more about a reliable partner in another country than a quarrelsome rival in the next city. That can be practical wisdom, a route into dependence or both.

The society need not be uniformly coastal to value maritime connections. Inland producers, transporters, financiers and towns are linked to exchanges they cannot personally see. The exact routes and ports await the geographic work. Names, languages and religious claims remain Kira’s own rather than direct copies of historical inscriptions. The Etruscan and Carthaginian references describe the resulting Estrusian culture, not an ancestry reserved for its humans. Desert Elven influence belongs to the culture’s formation; the histories of particular old gods, words and artistic forms remain to be developed.

### Gods, ancestors and the public bargain

The Concord of the Veil is Estru’s principal family of civic and ancestral cults. Its temples preserve ritual books, watch for auspices, honour city patrons and negotiate the relationship between household obligations and public necessity. Rites of memory and interpretation can give a temple great authority over whether a proposed alliance appears legitimate.

Desert Elven households helped shape the Concord alongside human households. Some participate in shared city cults, some preserve older family rites, and some combine civic obligations with Turning Bough practice. No named god is declared an exclusively human or Desert Elven import. A temple’s founding narrative can become contentious when it remembers a wealthy Sadire donor and forgets the families who maintained its rites.

This religious culture creates an important political resource. A wealthy house may endow a sanctuary, a league may seek a shared auspice and a foreign patron may ask for sacred endorsement of a contract. The existence of divination does not make every claimed sign truthful, every priest corrupt or every civic decision a supernatural certainty.

The Shared Table is a popular movement within this larger religious world. Its insistence on feeding those unable to repay can appeal to artisans, displaced people and trading households that remember precarious beginnings. It can also challenge temple patrons who demand gratitude as the price of relief.

### Defence and a future not owned by the neighbours

Defence follows the pressures of a divided political society. Defence relies on locally maintained troops, fortified places, hired specialists, crossbowmen and spears, with organization varying among cities and leagues. A temporary league may draw on substantial skill while struggling to maintain a single purpose.

Estrusian opinion can therefore be fierce and contradictory. A smith seeks foreign training to reduce future dependence. A merchant accepts an unequal agreement to keep a city alive. A wealthy nationalist makes money from the relationship denounced in his speeches. A veteran opposes another war because he loves the people who will have to fight it.

Estru’s great question is how to build enough common power to resist its neighbours without handing its own people to a new master at home. Kieran’s connections can help it obtain a hearing. They can also make him look like another famous outsider who arrives with a plan before learning what the people of the room have already tried.

## 8. Religions: what people owe the living and the dead

The faiths of Larius disagree about the making of the world, the nature of the soul and the final destination of the dead. This book describes those beliefs as beliefs. It does not establish that one priesthood possesses a complete and objectively proven cosmology. Magic can produce experiences that people interpret religiously without settling which interpretation is correct.

The religious traditions below shape the continent’s public and private life. Their adherents are not confined by ancestry. A Sadire can keep an Akarian hearth rite; a dwarf can honour a maritime vow; an elf can reject the religion of her household. Public observance, private conviction and institutional allegiance are different things.

### The First Hearth

The oldest Akarian tradition teaches that the world was once weight without shelter. Orun, called the Bearer, placed his shoulders beneath the first roof so that others could build a home. Dema, Keeper of the Last Ember, preserved the fire when the first builders quarrelled. Their stories make creation an act of sustaining rather than an act of ownership.

The First Hearth’s principal duties are to bear one’s share, leave sound work for those who follow and sustain the households beneath the ancestral roof. Imperial liturgy gives these duties splendid public form: processions of ancestral standards, ranked Keepers, consecrated work and blessings upon the empire’s arms. Court teachers may claim that the dwarves alone have understood the Bearer’s burden properly. The practical boundary of their generosity can coincide suspiciously well with the boundary of accepted Akarian belonging. Hearth halls maintain memorial tablets, mediate obligations and organize help after disaster. Skilled work can be an offering, but prosperity alone is not proof of divine favour. A magnificent forge with unsafe conditions can be condemned as an offence against the Bearer.

Its clergy are called Keepers. Some know engineering or medicine; others are teachers, archivists or carers. Dwarves do not possess spellcasting ability. No magical spellcasting is required for dwarven religious office. A Keeper who claims a miracle has made a religious claim, not demonstrated a new exception to the distinction between religious testimony and spellcasting.

Two tendencies shape its arguments. Foundation Keepers stress the obligations inherited from ancestors and the need to protect common structures. Open Hearth reformers argue that a foundation exists to support those alive now and can require rebuilding. Both contain humane teachers and people who use sacred language to protect their own authority.

Funeral rites name the work a person left behind, including care that produced no saleable object. The memorial of a poor carer may be read with greater honour than that of a rich owner. The worst spiritual accusation is not failure but concealment of a danger one knew others would bear.

### The Turning Bough

The Turning Bough is a family of elven traditions rather than one obedient hierarchy. Its best-known sacred figure is Ael, the Unwithering: understood by some as a deity, by others as the living pattern through which all things grow, change and pass beyond themselves. The bough is important because it bears fruit and new growth, not because it preserves every leaf forever.

Its established hierarchies can claim that the bough’s different branches express inherited duties and precedence. Sea-elf patrons emphasize magical stewardship; Plains and Tree teachers insist on the peer standing their communities already possess. A Mountain veteran may ask why a sacred account of mutual dependence honours his service more readily than his voice. These are disputes within a religion, not proof that its deity endorses one social arrangement.

Its old stories speak of several houses of existence: what has form, what can be remembered, what might yet become and what has passed beyond recall. They are theological images, not a supplied map that an adventurer can automatically traverse. Local teachers disagree about whether the houses describe places, states of the soul or ways of attending to the world.

The tradition’s strength is a long practice of remembrance. Its danger is the temptation to turn memory into ownership. A teacher may insist that the dead are honoured when the living are allowed to change; an aristocratic patron may prefer a doctrine in which the preferences of honoured ancestors decide every public question.

Observances vary with community and landscape. A sea household may release a braided cord into a returning tide. A mountain community may maintain a shelter in a dead relative’s name. A desert household may remember an ancestor by keeping water available to a traveller. A public grove is one expression of the faith, not proof that every elf must worship trees. Among Ice Elven observances, keeping a sheltered place ready for a returning traveller expresses continuity without requiring a living grove in unsuitable terrain. Desert Elven practice in Estru can overlap with civic ancestral rites; households need not abandon one inherited responsibility to acknowledge another. Practice varies by household and community; ancestry does not compel religious allegiance.

Its Feast of Returning Names gathers old accounts alongside the testimony of the young. In some towns the newest household tells its story first. In others, venerable families have quietly turned that rule into another occasion to speak about themselves. Reformers cite the original purpose with irritating accuracy.

### The Lantern Covenant

The Lantern Covenant is widespread in Sadion and present far beyond its borders. It honours Serath, Keeper of the Hearth and Road, who is said to have divided her first light so that no village would need to wait for a king before helping its neighbour. The faith values protection, hospitality, truthful witness and the duty to seek people who have not come home.

Lantern houses provide beds for travellers, care for orphans and maintain channels through which news of the missing can travel. Their institutional wealth differs enormously. A rich urban house may own productive property and lend to merchants; a poor rural one may depend on a widow who brings flour whenever she can spare it.

The Roman–Holy Roman design foundation gives its public establishment a strong ceremonial presence. Standards receive blessings, units keep patronal observances and major houses compete to endow shrines that remember their service. A captain can sincerely understand discipline as a sacred obligation while a village Keeper asks why the men who returned wounded have not received the help promised under those same banners.

The relationship with the crown is close but contested. Court clergy describe kingship as stewardship under the Lantern. Independent houses answer that stewardship can be judged by the people it fails. A royal officer may expect religious endorsement of a levy and instead receive a list of widows still waiting for the last promised relief.

Grad, San and other national figures can be honoured as exemplary mortals. Whether particular rulers deserve such veneration is debated. Royal success supplies no universal claim to godhood or divine approval for the Vollan line.

The Night of Open Doors is kept when the local community can support it, without selecting a universal calendar date. Lamps are placed where a lost traveller could see them; households offer a meal; the names of the missing are read without pretending that remembrance proves death. Soldiers’ families often find that final distinction essential.

### The Open Horizon

The Open Horizon is especially influential in Kyrinus. It reveres Ilyr, the Unmoored, who is said to have taught the first voyagers that a promise freely made creates a road where no road existed. Its teachers reject the idea that inherited authority can make a vow on behalf of every future generation.

Its sacred concerns are departure, return, consent and responsibility beyond familiar shores. A good voyage is not measured only by what comes home in its holds. The treatment of a stranger, a hired hand or a people met abroad belongs in the account. This gives the tradition a direct interest in the western expedition and an uncomfortable history of its own when commerce has fallen short of its teaching.

Horizon houses witness agreements, support stranded travellers and hold accounts for families separated by distance. Witnessing does not make an unjust contract sacred. Priests and lay navigators argue publicly over whether particular obligations were freely accepted, especially when one party had no reasonable way to refuse.

Some adherents see independence from Everwood as a collective act of religious adulthood. Others warn that turning the faith into a national possession betrays its recognition of obligations across borders. A foreigner can be a respected member of a Horizon house without adopting Kyrinian political loyalties.

### The Concord of the Veil

The Concord is a family of Estrusian civic and ancestral cults, shaped around the setting’s Etruscan and Carthaginian cultural influences. Its sacred world is one of city patrons, inherited ritual books, signs, reciprocal obligations and the continued presence of the dead in household memory. Its temples can be wealthy and politically important without every devotee belonging to a single centralized church.

Five divine names recur in the Concord: Velara, guardian of gates and fair bargains; Cathren, keeper of ships and safe returns; Eshra, warden of the honoured dead; Thalan, bearer of storm and public warning; and Mereth, giver of ripening and household increase. Their worshippers disagree about the patrons’ nature, demands and relationship to the wider sacred world. Cities and families may favour different patrons.

Diviners compare signs with inherited interpretations, while temple scholars argue over who may explain a disputed omen. An auspice can guide a community, be misunderstood or be recruited into a political argument. The book does not make every ritual a functioning prophecy or import an entire historical religion into Kira.

Painted burial chambers, ancestor feasts, bronze votives and endowed sanctuaries make household prestige visible. Trading families link local cults to partners abroad; a temple may become both a source of relief and a centre of debt. Estru’s dependence on stronger neighbours gives those institutions particular importance: when secular power is divided, a shared sacred undertaking can help a league act together or help its most ambitious patron speak for everyone.

### The Shared Table

The Shared Table grew among Estrusian households for whom grand promises repeatedly ended in someone else’s settlement. It is a popular movement within a society shaped by the Concord’s civic and ancestral cults, with some links to the Sadire world of the Lantern Covenant. It has no universally recognized founder or single supreme house.

Its best-loved figure is Maera of the Bread Lines, a folk saint remembered for refusing to separate the hungry into the deserving and the politically convenient. Local stories disagree about where she lived and whether Maera was her name. Those variations are part of the tradition rather than evidence that every village has secretly preserved the same official biography.

Table gatherings begin by making space for someone who cannot repay the meal. They teach that hunger can turn a nominal agreement into coercion. The faith therefore concerns itself with labour, debt, displacement and the obligations of a powerful guest. It has a reputation for politics because helping people survive often becomes a question of who controls the stores.

Its assemblies also produce quarrels, patronage and exclusion. A community can congratulate itself on generosity while silencing the stranger seated at its table. The strongest reformers insist that receiving food must not purchase the receiver’s political obedience.

### The Keepers of the Last Door

The Last Door is a transnational funerary fellowship whose members retain other religious allegiances. It claims no monopoly on the afterlife. Its compact concerns what the living may do with the dead: identify them honestly, preserve their dignity, acknowledge uncertain deaths and resist the use of a body or spirit as property.

The fellowship’s members keep burial registers, attend disaster sites and mediate disputes over remains. They include mortuary workers, healers, archivists and clergy from several traditions. Some refuse all attempts to restore the dead. Others permit an attempt only where the person’s wishes, identity and circumstances can be responsibly established.

They differ sharply over Kieran. One Keeper may respect a healer who has seen the cost of failure. Another may fear the authority of a man celebrated for returning life. Their question is not merely whether he can perform a wonder. It is who can refuse him, who can challenge his judgement, and whether a grateful family can bear to hear that an attempt has failed.

### The Doctrine of Continuance

Continuance is a small but influential religious and philosophical movement arguing that preventable death is a social failure. Its serious adherents fund healing, preservation of knowledge and better access to care. They ask why ordinary people should accept losses that the powerful spend fortunes avoiding.

Its dangerous wing treats consent, identity and grief as obstacles to eventual perfection. Some supporters want Kieran to teach everything he knows; some want to possess what they imagine he knows. He can agree with their attack on unequal access while rejecting the belief that sufficient power will make every loss reversible.

The movement should not be reduced to a secret cult whose members are all villains. A careful researcher may save lives. A grieving patron may fund cruelty. A demagogue can use the language of compassion while deciding that other people will provide its costs.

## 9. Magic, mortality and a world that must still function

Firearms, industrial craft and powerful magic coexist in Larius. The setting presents their social consequences without selecting new IRGS damage values, costs, durations or resolution rules.

Magical education is uneven. Some knowledge belongs to households and teachers, some to institutions, some to people whose discoveries do not fit a respectable curriculum. Talent, access, time and practice all matter. A grand title on a character sheet does not imply that every practitioner can achieve the same result under every condition.

The Ice Elves’ superior magic does not settle every problem that keeps them isolated. Magical capability, knowledge of a particular problem, a viable habitat and the right to remain there are distinct advantages. No universal ability to remake climate or disregard dwarven control follows from their strength. Likewise, faster maturation supplies no new spell progression or automatic comparison between an individual Ice Elf and Kieran.

Ordinary healing and extraordinary restoration are different social experiences. A healer who reliably treats common injuries can transform a community even if they cannot return the dead. Communities still require sanitation, food, shelter and trained carers. Everwood’s ancient disease catastrophe remains a lesson in the danger of mistaking natural or magical strength for knowledge of every illness.

Kieran’s listed resurrection and raising of the dead do not make death trivial. Attempting restoration does not itself establish that the same person has returned, that return was possible or that it was freely wanted. Preservation of a body does not guarantee preservation of a person. A moving corpse is not proof that the dead inhabit it. These principles shape how restoration is understood; its detailed supernatural mechanism remains open.

Different traditions describe the limit differently. The Turning Bough speaks of a name passing beyond recall. Horizon teachers speak of a journey that cannot be commanded backward. A secular scholar may discuss a broken continuity of mind and memory. Their uncertainty permits real theological argument without requiring every witness to deny what magic visibly does.

Firearms are equally consequential without being universally available. They depend on material, skilled manufacture, maintenance, training and supply. A country that imports finished weapons may gain battlefield strength and acquire a new dependence. A workshop producing excellent tools can help a rival become less dependent over time. Enchanting and engineering can cooperate, compete or simply solve different problems.

Dwarves’ lack of spellcasting does not mean that they lack souls, religious insight, imagination or the capacity to understand another person’s craft. Characters may assert those prejudices; the world description does not endorse them. Whether dwarves can operate particular enchanted objects remains an explicit unresolved rules question.

## 10. Common life across the borders

People usually encounter another culture through work, kinship, food and inconvenience before they encounter its national philosophy. A carrier learns which bridge official accepts a late explanation. A healer knows where a colleague will take a patient without payment. A travelling actor discovers which national joke gets a laugh and which ends the performance.

Trade runs through overlapping chains rather than one universal market. Akarian metalwork and firearms, Evalian timber and fine craft, Sadion’s agricultural and maritime networks, Kyrinian shipping and Southern Estru’s rare materials form broad relationships of exchange. They establish no continent-wide monopoly, precise production quantity or guaranteed route. Poor roads, incompatible measures, damaged tools and disputed tolls make ordinary expertise valuable.

Merchants often use a working trade speech while preserving household and regional languages. This edition names no new ancient language as a compulsory racial trait and treats fluency as learned experience. A court interpreter may understand an insult that a fluent traveller misses because the words refer to a local historical dispute.

Marriage, adoption and chosen household ties are all visible in Larius. Law differs by jurisdiction and agreement. Where different lifespans meet, inheritance and care can become difficult without making affection impossible. A contract that once seemed generous may be inadequate a generation later; a household may revise it because its members wish to live together rather than because one ancestry has a predetermined view of love.

Children inherit stories, not automatic loyalties. Formal schooling is most accessible in cities, organized institutions and prosperous households. Apprenticeship and community teaching spread useful knowledge more widely. Priests, veterans and travelling performers can teach accurate history or repeat a convenient falsehood with equal confidence.

Clothing makes practical distinctions before symbolic ones: workshop protection, weather, travel and the need to carry tools. Ceremony adds local choices. Akarian formal dress displays the hand that made or repaired an object; Evalian gatherings may favour a visible contribution from someone else; Sadire hosts often show hospitality through abundance; Kyrinian dress can display things received on journeys; an Estrusian table may value an ordinary object repaired until its history becomes apparent.

These are tendencies and storytelling resources, not uniforms. A person who dresses against local expectation may be poor, fashionable, devout, rebellious or merely cold.

Several public observances travel across borders. The First Opening commemorates the Ascension, celebrated in Akaria and interpreted more cautiously elsewhere. A shared Peace Vigil remembers the dead of the long war while each locality chooses which names it finds hardest to read. Independence observances in Kyrinus and Estru can be occasions for joy, argument or awkward visits by neighbours. No month lengths or exact annual festival dates are selected here.

Songs preserve people excluded from state histories. A regiment’s proud march may share a tune with a widow’s lament; a comic verse can expose a minister’s claim to ancient precedent. Kieran sometimes hears himself made into a figure he does not recognize. Correcting the singer is not always the kindest or most effective response.

### Other peoples of Larius

The creator confirms that the peoples already described are the dominant populations and leaders of the principal nations, not a complete list of the continent’s inhabitants. Other minor races exist with varying intelligence and sentience. Their smaller political footprint does not make them decorative fauna or permit every national claim to land to be treated as uncontested.

The Sivren are river and wetland dwellers whose complex oral accounts preserve navigation, seasonal use and agreements between communities. Their lack of familiar state paperwork can leave them exposed when a kingdom announces that an apparently unowned bank is available for a grant. They are fully reasoning people whose knowledge is expressed differently, not a population made less intelligent by the absence of a Legion or senate.

The Keld are small-bodied inhabitants of fissures and narrow stone country, a people distinct from Akarian clans. Their settlement knowledge and careful material culture give them interests that can conflict with imperial expansion. A dwarven account of discovering usable ground might be a Keld account of a place becoming dangerous. Their existence is not identified with the ancient Karthi encounter, whose participants remain a separate historical question.

The Vesh are a social people whose communication relies heavily on collective sound, movement and repeated patterns. The extent of individual self-awareness and the relation between individual and group intention remain undeveloped. Different interactions may reveal capacities unfamiliar to a scholar trained to look for a particular kind of speech. Neither an uncomprehending observer nor an apparent imitation supplies a final answer about their sentience.

These sketches make room for different kinds of minds without supplying a ladder of moral value or declaring that all minor peoples have one legal status. Further authoring must establish their biology, communities, relationships and actual capacities before giving them live records or mechanical classifications. The dominant states may dispute recognition; the setting should distinguish those political judgements from what has actually been established.

## 11. Landscape, creatures and the limits of the map

The Larius map supplies useful terrain, settlements and symbols, but not a reliable world-distance scale or a complete climate model. This expansion therefore builds local texture around known places without fixing travel times, inventing a new coastline or moving disputed settlements.

The snow country associated with the Akarian surface supports the Bracatis, or Battle Rams. Their military prestige does not make every animal a war mount or every dwarf a rider. Breeding, herding, handling and feed provide livelihoods of their own. Malachi is exceptional because of his bond with Cathius and his place in the emperor’s story.

Everwood’s forests are worked landscapes as well as places of wonder. Roads, gathering rights, old cultivation, memorial groves and disputed woodland can overlap. A community may protect an ancient stand because it supports water and livelihoods, because ancestors are remembered there, or because a powerful family would prefer that nobody inspect what lies beneath it.

Three new creatures provide ordinary ecological texture. Cinder moths gather around sustained warmth and can foul the ventilation of an ill-kept workshop. Glass eels live in particular subterranean pools and are valued by some artisans for their translucent remains. Hollow stags are wary woodland animals whose calls carry unexpectedly through old timber. None is automatically a demon, an alchemical cure or a reliable omen. Their distribution, rarity and practical effects require local authoring before they become gameplay facts.

Danger often comes from familiar systems under strain: a neglected shaft, a flooded store, contaminated water, a predator displaced by logging or a road made unsafe by people protecting an illicit trade. Spectacular threats can exist, but a living continent should not need a new ancient evil to explain every broken promise.

## 12. Institutions between the nations

The Rangers of Larius are the best-known institution able to move among several states without being identical to any one of them. Their independence is always negotiated. Permission to cross a border, carry arms, investigate a complaint or transport a prisoner must come from the circumstances and the relevant authority, rather than from the fame of their leader.

The League of Safe Roads is an association of carriers, hostel keepers, merchants and communities interested in keeping routes usable. It funds repairs and reports hazards, but its priorities favour roads its members use. A neglected village can reasonably ask why the league’s language of universal safety stops at a profitable junction.

The Witness Houses preserve copies of agreements and testimony in several jurisdictions. They sell dependable custody and comparison of documents. Their great virtue is the possibility that a powerful patron cannot destroy every copy. Their weakness is the temptation to treat access to an archive as a private service when its contents govern public lives.

The Lantern houses, Hearth Leagues, Last Door fellowship and local assemblies create further connections across borders. A crisis may be addressed through them when formal diplomacy is too slow. Their members still have interests, prejudices and legal limits. Cooperation is an accomplishment, not a universal power granted by good intentions.

The central institutional question for Kieran is whether the Rangers will become something able to survive his judgement. A company that does good only because one exceptional man always knows what to do has a fragile future. A company with accountable practices may sometimes reach a decision he dislikes.

## 13. The present tensions

### The expedition charter

Negotiation of the westward fleet’s charter is a central continental story. Who may speak for the expedition? What happens when a sponsor’s interests differ from an agreement made abroad? Can a ranger refuse an order that a financier believes was already purchased? Does a place found by Larius automatically become something Larius may claim?

Akaria and Southern Estru are connected to the offer in Kieran’s record. Other interests may seek participation, information, guarantees or the opportunity to stop a competitor. The destination remains unspecified. TalmahKah, Aldain, Azaloth, Falkion and Bendara are not silently assigned as targets or assumed to be unknown to their own inhabitants.

### The names absent from the peace

A proposed dispute concerns surviving copies of the settlement of 400 A.A. Different versions appear to account differently for some displaced communities. The premise creates an investigation, not proof that every old treaty was forged. Mistake, damaged records, selective copying and deliberate exclusion are all possibilities to establish through later authoring or play.

Kieran’s memories matter, but recollection is not an all-purpose answer. A person may remember the day of a surrender and still not know what a minister negotiated elsewhere. A respected witness can have been misled.

### The cost of the new workshops

Trade and manufacture create prosperity while shifting risk. An unsafe factory, an exploitative supply contract or a town dependent on one purchaser can bring the states’ public promises into conflict. This story can involve sincere reformers and responsible employers alongside cynical ones. It should not require every inventor to be reckless or every worker to agree on the remedy.

### A republic’s living royal descendant

Kieran’s lineage becomes newly useful to people who want to legitimize political change. A royalist can seek restoration, a republican can seek his endorsement and an outside power can threaten to recognize a claim he has never made. His refusal to be a king does not automatically stop others from spending political capital on the possibility.

### Who inherits the Rangers?

The company’s founding refugees included several peoples. Its present membership is recorded as elven only. Descendants of its mortal founders can honour that history while asking why a story about common rescue now authorizes exclusion. Existing Rangers may fear losing the practices that hold the company together. Kieran must face the question without a predetermined answer to the company’s membership question.

### The demand that nobody die

Continuance patrons offer support for healing research and then ask what access that support has bought. The Last Door fellowship presses for accountable practice. Bereaved families ask for help that neither institution can promise. Kieran’s reputation makes silence a public statement, but speaking honestly may disappoint people who need hope more urgently than an explanation.

These tensions have no scheduled victories, forced betrayals or guaranteed wars. Each can develop through the independent priorities of its people. Kieran is central because he is connected to the questions, not because nothing can happen until he arrives.

## 14. Places worth knowing

These places anchor the continent’s political and personal histories. Their exact coordinates and measured routes remain to be developed.

### Karkaria

The imperial underground capital should feel like a city made through successive decisions rather than excavated in one miraculous act. Old routes, prestigious halls and later works coexist. A new vent may provoke a more consequential argument than a court ceremony. The memorial tablets include the names of people who died maintaining the city, and arguments about which names were left out. Karwathis is the superseded map label for this capital; the current name is Karkaria.

### Forlathis

Kieran’s birthplace lives with visitors who want to find the beginning of his legend. Its inhabitants have ordinary reasons to care about rent, work and local decisions. A proposed public commemoration of his parents has become a disagreement about which version of the republican founding the town will endorse. Kieran can be invited to speak without being entitled to settle it.

### Tethris and Evalia

The former and present capitals carry different kinds of prestige. Tethris preserves the social memory of the monarchy and the inconvenience of being treated as a place whose importance ended. Evalia houses republican ceremony, argument and the work of governing. Families, artists and scholars move between them. Old royal associations do not make every resident of Tethris a restorationist; residence in the republican capital does not prove a commitment to equal representation.

### Bay Watch

The capital of Sadion gives the kingdom’s protective claims a visible stage: petitions, naval business, provisioning, court display and people waiting for an answer. The setting gives its waterfront a strong culture of collective practical knowledge. A clerk may know which cargo can wait; a dock worker may know why it cannot. When the two trust one another, a crisis can be solved before anyone makes a speech.

### Griffin

Griffin should contain the possibility of a life that Daniel’s childhood did not offer him. Crowded lodging houses, workshops, small traders, neighbourhood kitchens and recruiting offices make it a place of opportunity and extraction. A successful former orphan can become a benefactor, a convenient public example or a person reminded that he has not repaired the conditions from which he escaped.

### Vlaris

Vlaris is a city of arrivals with a long memory of departures. Its meaning to Kieran includes the anonymous life he tried to make after the war. Workshops and households retain private recollections that do not resemble official biography. The public revelation of an old friendship could be moving for one participant and intrusive for the other.

### Cthulis

Cthulis carries the memory of siege, capture and resistance. The expanded city is neither permanently ruined nor perfectly restored. Its newer work stands over older foundations; families differ over which traces should remain visible. Someone whose home is inconveniently protected as a war memorial may have an understandable view of commemorative enthusiasm.

### Estru City

The map gives Estru City prominent standing. The setting presents it as a place where the meaning of independence is argued through contracts, employment and the control of civic spaces. Public meals and foreign warehouses belong to the same streets. The city’s exact present constitutional rank remains unselected; the wider Estrusian political settlement remains to be developed.

### Castle LuCain

The castle anchors the expedition thread without requiring a new war to begin at its gates. It is a useful setting for negotiations among sponsors, hired specialists, carriers, religious witnesses and people seeking passage. Its authority over the expedition must be established by agreement, not inferred from the fact that everyone has gathered there. The castle’s jurisdiction remains among the geography questions to resolve.

### Ikarion

Ikarion, spelled Ikrarion in older accounts, passed to Everwood during the war. Its households carry different memories of the same settlement: a claim recognized, a home retained under another flag, or a voice excluded from the agreement. It offers a setting for arguments about title, inherited residence and who was entitled to negotiate for the people who remained.

### Dole’s March, Fort Corinth and SunWatch

These map names offer three local perspectives beyond the capitals. Dole’s March can develop the encounter between military memory and the people supplying it. Fort Corinth can place public safety against the priorities of those living near a guarded route. SunWatch can explore the meeting of Akarian surface life and traditions formed underground. These are suggested local emphases, not newly enacted garrison orders or confirmed administrative arrangements.

### Nucarius’ Tower

Nucarius’ Tower is associated with observation, copied records and the comparison of what different witnesses believe they saw. Scholars of weather and the sky work alongside keepers of older testimony. Their instruments and disagreements offer knowledge with limits, rather than an omniscient view of the continent. Its most useful secret may be a neglected piece of ordinary evidence.

## 15. New recurring people

The following people belong to the setting’s supporting cast. Their work, loyalties and disagreements continue beyond Kieran’s presence. Their first scenes remain open to the particular campaign or story.

Lethra Senn is an Evalian archivist who admires the republic enough to be angered by its evasions. She wants important agreements made publicly intelligible. Kieran’s memory interests her, but she refuses to cite his reputation as a substitute for corroboration. She can become a trusted collaborator precisely because she is prepared to discover that he is mistaken.

Dorrun Valehammer is an Akarian works inspector committed to making industrial expansion defensible to the people bearing its risks. He respects Cathius without assuming that imperial goodwill prevents negligence. A Hearth League may regard him as an ally on one project and a defender of the owners on another. He wants standards that survive a change of patron.

Rovan Kest is a Sadire former quartermaster who carries petitions from soldiers’ households. He knows the temptation to redirect an inadequate supply and defend the decision afterward. His loyalty to the kingdom is real. So is his willingness to embarrass its officers when they prefer ceremonial gratitude to the delivery of promised help.

Neris Tal is a Kyrinian navigator and negotiator associated with an Open Horizon house. She wants an expedition charter that can survive contact with people whose consent its sponsors have not yet sought. She will respect Kieran’s experience and still ask when he last accepted terms set by somebody less famous than himself.

Vessa Morn organizes a Shared Table in Southern Estru and earns her living keeping accounts for households that cannot afford mistakes. She refuses to choose between feeding people now and questioning the arrangement that keeps them dependent. Foreign patrons find her useful until she applies the same scrutiny to their generosity as to their contracts.

Sera Vael is an elven Ranger who manages practical company affairs: supplies, apprenticeships, care and the memory of promises. She believes the company needs procedures that can constrain its founder. Her loyalty is tested when admirers of Kieran treat every request for accountability as an insult to his sacrifices.

Taren Ash is a healer associated with the Last Door fellowship. He supports careful restoration research while insisting that a successful outcome does not prove a justified method. He can defend Kieran against a false accusation and challenge him over a real failure in the same conversation. His disagreement is most effective when it comes from shared concern rather than fear of all magic.

Each person needs relationships, resources and limits beyond the central character. Vessa has a community to answer to; Dorrun has colleagues and cases; Neris has obligations to people who may never meet a Ranger. They should continue to have reasons to act when Kieran is elsewhere.

## 16. Kieran and The Price of Peace

The separate [Kieran dossier](KIERAN_PELLEAS.md) develops his interior life, public roles, relationships, failings, exceptional longevity and present arc. The organizing idea is that the continent’s main character can matter enormously without being the continent’s owner.

The Price of Peace is the setting’s central continuing arc. It connects the western charter, the treatment of old testimony, the Rangers’ membership and succession, industrial change and the politics of restoration. These are several problems with their own causes, not a hidden single conspiracy that explains every disagreement.

Kieran’s old strategy is to carry the danger himself, hold the painful truth until the right moment and use personal trust to bridge institutions that do not trust one another. That strategy has saved people. It has also left too many arrangements dependent on one person’s judgement.

The question is whether he can distribute responsibility without abandoning care. Other people must be allowed to know enough to choose, to disagree without losing his protection, and to build institutions he cannot personally supervise forever. A satisfying story can make him less indispensable while making his contribution more lasting.

This arc needs no predetermined ending. He might learn to release authority, become more controlling under pressure, suffer a failure, find an unexpected partner or lose the chance to resolve a question himself. The world remains able to continue. Its people inherit what he actually did rather than what a legend promised he would do.

## 17. Canon and further development

This expanded worldbook and the Kieran dossier establish the current Larius setting, as adopted by the creator in KIRA-U06. The earlier documents remain an archive of the world’s development and have no authority to overrule this edition. The creator’s national inspirations, caste relationships, Desert and Ice Elven details and separate continental shelves remain foundations of the setting.

The reference present is 1342 A.A. The continental war spans 80–400 A.A.; Kieran’s active service accounts for 228 years within it. Independence belongs to 500 A.A. And Estru’s later invasion to 600 A.A. Kieran’s Long Renewal accounts for his exceptional age. Karkaria, Evalia, Ikarion and Daniel Convor are the current names; superseded spellings stay in the archive and map transcription.

The eight religious traditions, developed national cultures, interregional institutions, supporting cast, creatures, Sivren, Keld and Vesh belong to this canon. A possible negotiation, investigation or future personal choice is still a story opening. Its inclusion does not establish a completed event or determine who succeeds.

Religious teachings remain beliefs held by people in the setting. They do not establish a fully known cosmology. Exact constitutions, unassigned caste positions, route distances and numerical magic rules remain further development. Existing campaign events retain their own history; the worldbook supplies no automatic change to a live campaign.
